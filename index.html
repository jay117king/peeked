The problem

Most goal apps treat humans like machines

Long lists ignore working memory, motivation science, and energy. Overwhelm wins — the goal doesn’t.

🧠

Working memory ignored

We hold only a few items in mind. Giant lists exceed capacity and create anxiety instead of clarity.

📉

No dopamine strategy

Motivation needs intermediate wins. Traditional apps give almost none — interest fades before the finish line.

⚡

One-size-fits-none

Rigid structures fail people with ADHD, shifting energy, or different learning styles.

The solution

Science meets game design

Peer-reviewed research on how brains finish goals — plus a progressive, low-pressure game layer.

  • Neurologically sized steps — Typically 15–45 minutes, aligned with working memory and ultradian rhythms.
  • Evidence-based sequencing — Goal-gradient effect, implementation intentions, spaced practice, deliberate practice.
  • Adaptive by design — Recalibrates from completion data, energy reports, and uploads.
  • Game layer without toxicity — XP, skill trees, flexible streaks, narrative — no shame mechanics.
  • Built for real brains — ADHD and executive-function support using clinical guidelines.
Core promise

Research foundations

Where the science comes from

Pathbreak’s design principles are drawn from peer-reviewed work in cognitive psychology, behavioral science, learning science, and clinical research on executive function. This is a transparent map of the research traditions behind the product — not a claim of clinical validation.

Working memory & cognitive load

Used for: Keeping micro-steps small enough that they don’t overwhelm attention.

  • Baddeley, A. D., & Hitch, G. (1974). Working memory. In G. H. Bower (Ed.), The psychology of learning and motivation (Vol. 8). Academic Press.
  • Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 24(1), 87–114.
  • Sweller, J. (1988). Cognitive load during problem solving: Effects on learning. Cognitive Science, 12(2), 257–285.

Implementation intentions & goal follow-through

Used for: Turning vague goals into concrete when / where / how next actions.

  • Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans. American Psychologist, 54(7), 493–503.
  • Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119.

Goal-gradient effect & progress motivation

Used for: Visible progress, XP, and milestones that increase engagement as goals get closer.

  • Hull, C. L. (1932). The goal-gradient hypothesis and maze learning. Psychological Review, 39(1), 25–43.
  • Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). The goal-gradient hypothesis resurrected: Purchase acceleration, illusionary goal progress, and customer retention. Journal of Marketing Research, 43(1), 39–58.

Self-determination & sustainable motivation

Used for: Autonomy, competence, and relatedness — avoiding shame-based streak systems.

  • Deci, E. L., & Ryan, R. M. (2000). The “what” and “why” of goal pursuits: Human needs and the self-determination of behavior. Psychological Inquiry, 11(4), 227–268.
  • Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2017). Self-determination theory: Basic psychological needs in motivation, development, and wellness. Guilford Press.

Deliberate practice & skill acquisition

Used for: Sequencing learning goals into focused practice with feedback.

  • Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. Psychological Review, 100(3), 363–406.
  • Ericsson, K. A., & Pool, R. (2016). Peak: Secrets from the new science of expertise. Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.

Spaced repetition & durable learning

Used for: Scheduling review so knowledge sticks instead of cramming.

  • Ebbinghaus, H. (1885/1913). Memory: A contribution to experimental psychology (H. A. Ruger & C. E. Bussenius, Trans.). Teachers College, Columbia University.
  • Cepeda, N. J., Pashler, H., Vul, E., Wixted, J. T., & Rohrer, D. (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis. Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380.
  • Kang, S. H. K. (2016). Spaced repetition promotes efficient and effective learning. Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 3(1), 12–19.

Desirable difficulties & interleaving

Used for: Mixing practice types and slightly harder challenges for long-term retention.

  • Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2011). Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way: Creating desirable difficulties to enhance learning. In M. A. Gernsbacher et al. (Eds.), Psychology and the real world. Worth.
  • Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2007). The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning. Instructional Science, 35(6), 481–498.

Focus blocks & ultradian rhythms

Used for: Preferring ~15–45–90 minute work units over marathon sessions.

  • Kleitman, N. (1963). Sleep and wakefulness (Rev. ed.). University of Chicago Press.
  • Cirillo, F. (2006). The Pomodoro Technique. (Popular application of short focused intervals.)

ADHD, executive function & accessible task design

Used for: Smaller steps, flexible recovery, low-shame accountability, reduced working-memory load.

  • Barkley, R. A. (2012). Executive functions: What they are, how they work, and why they evolved. Guilford Press.
  • Barkley, R. A. (2015). Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder: A handbook for diagnosis and treatment (4th ed.). Guilford Press.
  • Solanto, M. V. (2011). Cognitive-behavioral therapy for adult ADHD: Targeting executive dysfunction. Guilford Press.

Habits, environment & behavior change

Used for: Stacking new actions onto existing routines and reducing friction.

  • Wood, W., & Neal, D. T. (2007). A new look at habits and the habit-goal interface. Psychological Review, 114(4), 843–863.
  • Clear, J. (2018). Atomic Habits. Avery. (Practitioner synthesis of habit research.)

Disclaimer: These sources inform product design principles (step size, sequencing, motivation, accessibility). Pathbreak is not a medical device and is not a substitute for clinical care. Citations will expand as the product and literature review mature.
